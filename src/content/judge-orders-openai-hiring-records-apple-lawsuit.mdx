## Judge Orders OpenAI to Turn Over Apple Hiring Records in Lawsuit

### Federal court grants Apple expedited discovery of recruitment documents and executive depositions in trade secrets dispute.

In a significant escalation of the legal battle between Silicon Valley giants, a federal judge has ruled that Apple will gain expedited access to OpenAI’s internal recruitment records and interview materials. The ruling comes as Apple pursues a high-stakes trade secrets lawsuit alleging that OpenAI systematically poached hardware engineering talent to siphon proprietary technologies for its unreleased consumer AI devices.

## Key Details

U.S. District Judge Edward Davila granted Apple expedited discovery requests targeting OpenAI’s recruitment activities for its consumer hardware and robotics divisions. Under the court order, OpenAI must produce documents associated with interview processes for former Apple personnel within strict deadlines—turning over existing records within five days and any remaining relevant files within 14 days.

The scope of the ordered discovery includes interview checklists, technical evaluation guides, internal briefing scripts, and presentation materials used during candidate evaluations. Notably, the court is requiring OpenAI to disclose interview records and communications that reference proprietary Apple technology, trade secrets, or unannounced hardware components.

In addition to document production, Judge Davila authorized Apple to conduct formal depositions of key former Apple employees currently working at OpenAI:
- **Tang Yew Tan**: Chief Hardware Officer at OpenAI and former senior Apple executive.
- **Chang Liu**: Hardware engineering leader at OpenAI, formerly at Apple.
- **Alyssa Peng**: Consumer hardware specialist at OpenAI, formerly at Apple.

The court also ordered OpenAI to turn over private messages transmitted via the LINE messaging app between the named individuals regarding their departure from Apple and recruitment to OpenAI. However, Judge Davila denied broader requests from Apple to inspect OpenAI's core research and development repositories for power management and metal finishes, allowing Apple the option to submit narrowed requests later.

### What This Means

The legal clash highlights the intense competition for specialized talent as AI foundation labs move beyond pure software models toward physical hardware ecosystem control. OpenAI's ambitious effort to build a standalone consumer AI device—designed to challenge smartphones and rethink personal computing—relies heavily on hardware engineering expertise traditionally concentrated within Apple.

Apple’s lawsuit, filed in July, alleges a deliberate pattern of intellectual property theft. Apple claims that departing engineers used corporate laptops to download confidential technical specifications, unreleased product roadmaps, and proprietary engineering presentations shortly before resigning. Furthermore, Apple alleges that former staff actively coached prospective recruits on which internal files to extract prior to job interviews. By securing expedited access to interview transcripts and private messaging logs, Apple aims to establish whether OpenAI's hiring pipeline actively incentivized trade secret exfiltration.

### Technical Breakdown

The expedited court order focuses on specific evidentiary categories to determine if technical IP was transferred during hiring:

- **Candidate Presentation Repositories**: Technical slides and whiteboards submitted by former Apple engineers during OpenAI technical interviews.
- **Inter-Candidate Messaging Logs**: Private communication channels, specifically encrypted LINE messaging threads, used to discuss job offers and file transfers.
- **Cross-Referenced Hardware Specs**: Internal recruitment records containing references to Apple's proprietary power efficiency and thermal management designs.

## Industry Impact

This court ruling sets a critical precedent for executive hiring and talent mobility across the artificial intelligence and hardware sectors. As AI labs race to build proprietary chips, robotics, and consumer devices, the boundary between general technical knowledge and protected trade secrets has become a major legal battlefield.

For OpenAI, complying with the tight discovery timeline and submitting senior executives like Tang Yew Tan to depositions creates operational friction during a crucial hardware development cycle. For the broader industry, the case underscores the growing legal risks of aggressive recruiting campaigns targeting legacy tech giants, signaling that courts are willing to grant rapid access to internal interview records when trade secret theft is alleged.

## Looking Ahead

With depositions scheduled and document production underway, the proceedings will likely reveal unprecedented insights into OpenAI’s hardware roadmap and recruitment practices. Observers will be closely watching whether the uncovered materials prompt Apple to seek preliminary injunctions against OpenAI's hardware product launches.

As artificial intelligence moves from software screens into physical devices, the battle for hardware dominance will be fought as much in federal courtrooms as in research laboratories.
